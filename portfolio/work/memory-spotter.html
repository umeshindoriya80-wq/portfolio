Project context

Analysts are responsible for enabling Spotter (AI agent) for hundreds of business users, including leadership teams that rely on accurate answers for decision-making. To make Spotter effective, analysts must teach it business context.

However, this context was fragmented across multiple surfaces, required ongoing manual maintenance, and was difficult to scale.

Memory introduced a new approach for capturing, organising, and scaling business knowledge — helping analysts provide richer context to Spotter with less manual effort.

Project deliverables

Platform Foundation

Established Memory as a scalable knowledge layer within ThoughtSpot.

Phase 1 Experience

Designed the end-to-end workflow for generating Memory from Liveboards.

Context

What is Spotter?

Spotter is a conversational AI agent that answers business questions in natural language and delivers insights from your data.

Who uses it

Business users, managers, and leadership who ask questions in natural language and act on the answers.

Who enables it

Analysts configure and manage Spotter, ensuring it provides accurate, reliable answers across the organisation’s data models.

Why Spotter needs context

Business context helps Spotter understand the data better and answer accurately. Analysts have to continuously add and manage context through instructions, reference questions, business terms, and more.

Analyst pain points

Today, analysts spend time creating and maintaining business context across multiple surfaces. As knowledge changes, keeping that context accurate becomes increasingly difficult.

Challenge What this means in practice Impact
Repetitive manual setup Instructions, Q&A mappings, business terms — written once, never updated. Duplicated across every data model. High effort
Context spread across multiple surfaces Four separate places to add context. No single view. If Spotter answers wrong, the analyst checks everything to find the gap. Hard to manage
Static instructions become outdated Business changes. Instructions don’t. Stale context actively misleads Spotter. Knowledge decay
Conflicting context accumulates Old and new instructions coexist. The system doesn’t resolve them. Reliability drops. Reduced AI trust
Heavy setup before enabling Spotter Analysts face a blank slate. No starting point. Many delay or never enable Spotter on new data models. Cold-start problem

Opportunity

→ Memory presents an opportunity to shift analysts away from maintaining instructions and toward teaching business knowledge.

→ Instead of manually updating rules and mappings, analysts can provide context directly, while AI organises, relates, updates, and maintains that knowledge over time.

Potential benefits

  • Less repetitive manual work
  • Easier updates when business knowledge changes
  • Reduced conflicts between old and new context
  • A single knowledge layer instead of fragmented surfaces
  • Faster Spotter setup and adoption

What should qualify as Memory?

We started with a broad landscape of concepts: short-term memory, long-term memory, instructions, knowledge sources, business rules, and conversational context.

Rather than adopting existing AI definitions, we narrowed the concept to fit the needs of ThoughtSpot analysts and enterprise data.

Outcome

A practical definition of Memory for ThoughtSpot

Context created by AI, managed by AI, and used by AI.

Analysts can contribute knowledge and suggest associations, but the organisation, relationships, and retrieval of memory remain AI-managed.

Stage 1: Defining Memory

Before designing anything, we had to define what Memory is

The biggest risk wasn’t building the wrong flow. It was building the wrong mental model. We started with questions, not wireframes.

Questions we had to answer first

01 — What qualifies?

Not everything an analyst knows belongs in Memory. We needed to separate durable business knowledge from transient context and task-specific instructions.

02 — Who owns it?

Is it personal to a user? Scoped to a data model? Shared across the organisation? Ownership determines trust, governance, and how Memory scales.

03 — How does it scale?

ThoughtSpot serves enterprises with hundreds of analysts and thousands of data models. The architecture had to work at that scale from day one.

How we found the answers

Competitive analysis

Consumer AI (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot) and enterprise AI (Tableau, Cursor, OpenAI Enterprise, Intercom Fin). Surfaced 5 memory types and multiple ownership levels no single product had fully solved.

Internal sessions with PM and champions

Cross-functional sessions with Product, Engineering, and internal Spotter champions to surface existing mental models — often conflicting — and align on a shared definition before any architecture was proposed.

With a shared definition in place, we had the foundation to explore what Memory architecture should look like inside ThoughtSpot — not as an adaptation of any existing product, but designed specifically for an analyst managing AI accuracy across an organisation’s data.

Stage 2: Memory Architecture

We explored multiple IA models and intentionally chose the simplest one

The natural instinct was to create a memory type for every use case. That instinct was wrong. In foundational systems, complexity compounds over time — every new source, workflow, and AI capability adds weight. We chose an architecture that could scale with the product rather than capture every edge case on day one.

The core question was not where memory comes from, but who owns it and who it should affect. We explored multiple ownership models before converging on a simpler approach.

Architecture evolution

01

Initial iteration

Why the first model failed

Org, Agent, Data Model, User, and Chat Session created multiple places to manage context — introducing the same fragmentation problem we were trying to solve.

02

Final simplified iteration

Tagging instead of hierarchy

We stopped using architecture layers to define scope. Memories could be targeted through tags and metadata — allowing one system to support many use cases without additional memory types.

Simple ownership, flexible scope

With only two ownership types, the model remained easy to understand while supporting future products, agents, and workflows through tagging rather than new architectural layers.

The final architecture

Central Memory

Shared · Org-wide

Shared organisational knowledge managed by analysts. Applied across data models unless explicitly scoped otherwise.

Sources: Liveboards · Reference Questions · Business Terms

Revenue definitions Reporting conventions Business rules

Personal Memory

Individual · User-scoped

User-specific preferences and corrections that only affect an individual’s Spotter experience.

Sources: Conversation signals · User corrections

Preferred metrics Personal shortcuts Individual corrections
Design principle

Every memory ultimately answers two questions: Who owns this knowledge? And who should be affected by it? By reducing the model to Central Memory and Personal Memory, analysts could understand it immediately while the platform retained flexibility through tagging and scope controls.

Key insight

Conversations represented the future vision of Memory. However, they also introduced the highest risk. At this stage we were still learning what should be remembered, how memories should be structured, how users would react to AI-generated memory, and how to prevent incorrect memory creation. Before allowing AI to learn directly from conversations, we needed a trusted foundation.

V1 Decision

Learning from existing trusted sources

1. Liveboards

Analyst-curated dashboards containing verified business knowledge — trusted, structured, and immediately available without any new data entry.

2. Reference Questions

The flow already exists. We will just save the output as Memory instead of static context.

3. Business Terms

The flow already exists. We will just save the output as Memory instead of static context.

Why Liveboards

Already curated

Analysts intentionally create and maintain Liveboards. Content is authored, not incidental.

Rich business context

Liveboards contain metrics, definitions, explanations, and business narratives directly valuable for AI understanding.

High trust

Content is already reviewed and validated before reaching business users. The organisation already trusts it.

Immediate value

Organisations already had large Liveboard libraries that could immediately become Memory — no blank slate.

Key decisions

HMW reduce the number of steps?

Merged scope definition and context authoring into a single step to reduce navigation overhead and keep analysts focused on the memory they were creating.

HMW make Liveboard selection easier?

Instead of a flat list, surfaced metadata and contextual signals that helped analysts quickly identify the right Liveboard.

HMW handle long memory-generation times?

Generation could take 15–20 minutes. Rather than forcing users to wait, we allowed them to leave the workflow and notified them when memory creation completed.

HMW help analysts review generated memory?

Raw memory output was difficult to consume. We surfaced a concise summary of key learnings instead of exposing the full generated structure.

HMW build trust in AI-generated memory?

Reviewing the summary provided visibility, but trust ultimately comes from verification. The workflow encouraged analysts to validate the result through actual Spotter interactions.

HMW connect memory creation to usage?

Added a direct path into Spotter so analysts could immediately test how the newly created memory affected responses.

Stage 6: Connecting to Ecosystem

Fitting Memory in the ThoughtSpot ecosystem

A Memory Sources page hidden in Data Workspace is only half the answer. Analysts who work primarily in Liveboards or Spotter chat needed to encounter Memory without navigating to find it. We created entry points across the product ecosystem.

Impact

What changed after Memory shipped

Adoption

2×increase in Memory creation among top customers

Within the last 15 days of May, Memory creation doubled across some of our most active customers, indicating growing adoption and continued investment in improving Spotter’s responses.

Validation

✓ Customers trusted Memory more than expected

Many customers treated Memory generated from Liveboards as highly reliable because it originated from one of their most trusted data assets. This validated our decision to start with Liveboards as the first Memory source.

✓ Conversation became the preferred management surface

Customers responded positively to the ability to update Memory directly from Spotter conversations, reinforcing the importance of keeping Memory close to the workflows where users already interact with AI.

Key Learnings After Launch

! Visibility matters as much as generation

Our initial focus was helping analysts create Memory. Customer feedback showed that understanding what is currently stored is equally important for building trust and confidence in AI responses.

! A dedicated management page is not enough

Many users primarily interact with Spotter through chat and never visit Memory management surfaces. Discoverability and management need to exist within the conversational workflow itself.

! Conversation proved to be the strongest entry point

As anticipated, users found Memory through conversations more naturally than through standalone pages. Introducing conversational entry points earlier could have accelerated awareness and adoption across customers.

Reflection

My Learnings from this project

01

Creating clarity before creating solutions

For the first few weeks, I wasn’t designing screens — I was collecting context. There were more questions than answers: What is Memory? Why does it exist? How should it work? At times, it felt difficult to show progress because the work was happening through exploration, alignment, and synthesis rather than visible designs.

Learning

Not every design problem starts with interfaces. Sometimes the most valuable contribution is creating clarity from ambiguity.

02

Designing in a moving landscape

While defining Memory, the AI industry was evolving almost weekly. New approaches, capabilities, and terminology were constantly emerging. Waiting for perfect certainty wasn’t an option. We had to make decisions, establish foundations, and ship while the landscape continued to change.

Learning

In fast-moving domains, speed and adaptability are as important as design quality. The goal is not to predict the future perfectly, but to build foundations that can evolve with it.
