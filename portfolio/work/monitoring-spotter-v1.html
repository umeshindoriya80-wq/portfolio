01Context

Who owns Spotter — and what they can't see.

The setup

One analyst is accountable for an AI everyone else uses.

Business users ask questions in plain language and get charts and insights back. An analyst enables Spotter for their team — and is accountable for its accuracy. If Spotter gets it wrong, that's on them.

The blind spot

Once it ships, the owner goes blind.

The primary lever analysts have to improve Spotter is coaching — refining the questions it knows, fixing gaps in its knowledge. But coaching only works if you know what’s failing. With no visibility into real conversations, analysts were guessing: which answers missed? Where were users dissatisfied? What coaching should be added? Without answers, the cycle stalled — quality stagnated and the analyst had no signal to act on.

This is how an AI feature quietly loses the trust it needs to get adopted: users stop asking, the analyst can’t prove it’s working, and the product loses its strongest case at renewal. Visibility wasn’t a reporting nicety — it was the prerequisite for the product to improve at all.

02Discovery

Analysts could see conversations existed — not which ones failed.

Everyone agreed visibility was needed. The open question was what analysts would actually do once they could see conversations. Three research tracks answered it — and surfaced the specific failures the design had to solve.

1

Customer conversations

Spoke with customers after they’d enabled Spotter. The request came up repeatedly: “Give us visibility into how Spotter is being used and whether it’s performing.” This wasn’t a nice-to-have — it was blocking confidence in the product.

2

Analyst interviews

Structured interviews with the people accountable for Spotter quality surfaced a consistent picture:

  • Analysts felt blind to customer interactions after launch.
  • They could access a flat list of hundreds of conversations — but had no efficient way to identify which ones needed attention.
  • To investigate an issue they often had to scan the list manually, find a conversation, then recreate the user’s question in Spotter to understand what went wrong.
  • They had no way to see feedback after Spotter was enabled.
  • They struggled to determine what coaching to add — or whether existing coaching was working.
  • They lacked confidence that coaching decisions were targeting the right problems.
3

Competitive research

Studied how enterprise products approach monitoring. The strongest ones didn’t just expose metrics — they helped users observe activity, assess its meaning, and take action. That three-part structure became the foundation of the monitoring framework.

04Framing the space

I defined monitoring as a loop: Observe → Assess → Act.

Competitive research gave me the frame: the strongest monitoring products help users observe activity, assess its meaning, and take action. I applied the same structure to the analyst’s job — three moves that repeat, each one feeding the next.

1

Observe — track what's happening

Surface how users actually interact with Spotter: questions asked, answers returned, follow-ups, thumbs up and down. Make the signal visible.

2

Assess — judge what it means

Read the signal for failures, gaps, and coaching needs. Separate a one-off miss from a pattern worth fixing.

3

Act — refine, then watch again

Update coaching to improve accuracy and adoption — and the loop restarts. Every action changes what the next observation shows.

05The audit

Some visibility existed — scattered across four places.

Mapping the current journeys surfaced the real problem. The pieces existed, but no one could see the whole picture from one place.

Analyst Liveboard

Engagement and adoption metrics — but as raw tabular data. Read-only, no deep-dive, no action.

Spotter coaching

Reference questions and business terms, added by analysts and suggested by users. Powerful, but low adoption and buried.

Decision 01Defining scope

V1 had to solve both the IA and the workflow — neither worked alone.

I framed V1 around two problems that had to be solved together: the monitoring data was fragmented across surfaces, and there was no defined workflow for acting on it. Fix the structure alone and you get a tidy place where nothing happens; fix the workflow alone and you get a feature no one can find. V1 had to do both.

The structure

Solve the IA — one pane of glass.

  • Bring every scattered surface into one place.
  • Directly answers the fragmentation problem.
The process

Solve the monitoring workflow.

  • Choose the right signals to monitor.
  • Help the analyst assess what they're seeing.
  • Help them take a meaningful action on it.
06IA exploration

Mapping the space before committing.

I worked the full surface before deciding where things lived — touchpoints, signal flows, feature clusters, then two competing structures. Early explorations treated coaching as a first-class area inside monitoring. The original structure was Observe → Assess → Coach, with coaching management living alongside the monitoring surface.

Decision 02Limiting V1 scope

I scoped coaching out of V1 to ship visibility first.

This was the single largest scope decision, and it was mine to make. Coaching showed up in every early IA exploration, and there was pressure to ship it alongside monitoring. I argued to keep it out of V1 as a standalone workflow — and held that line.

Why I deferred coaching
  • The coaching space was changing rapidly. Memory was beginning to reshape how coaching might work.
  • The long-term architecture was unclear — premature investment risked building the wrong system.
  • V1 needed to establish visibility first. Coaching decisions without data to ground them would be guesswork.
What I kept in V1
  • The full monitoring surface — observe and assess.
  • The ability to add coaching inline from a conversation — the minimal action, surfaced in context.
  • A real signal to return from: V1 would generate the feedback needed to shape coaching properly in the next phase.
Decision 03Where monitoring lives

I chose one account-level monitor over per-model monitors.

Option A · per model

PROS

  • Data and monitoring sit at the same level.
  • Security scoped to that model's admin.

CONS

  • Page clutters as monitoring grows.

08Before & after

What the design changes for the analyst.

The clearest read on the design is the workflow it replaces. Here is what investigating a single failing answer took before — and what it takes now.

Before — reconstructing the failure
  • Open the adoption Liveboard and scan a flat log of questions and answers for a thumbs-down.
  • Copy the question, paste it into Spotter, and check whether the answer comes back wrong.
  • But your run doesn’t carry the user’s chat context — so it might look fine, or fail for a different reason.
  • Tedious, and still no certainty about why it failed for the user.
After — see it, correct it, coach it
  • Open the Spotter monitoring page; filter by the signal you need, or search for it.
  • Open the conversation as a full mirror — the user’s real context, with their feedback highlighted on the failing answer.
  • Correct that answer in place if it’s wrong, and save the fix as coaching.
  • One question–answer mapping, corrected at the source — and applied for every user.
Why it works

The mirror reproduces the user’s actual conversation, not an approximation. The analyst finally sees why the answer failed for that specific person — and fixes the root cause in one place, saving a corrected question–answer pair as coaching everyone benefits from. The single tedious loop that produced no certainty becomes one confident action.

09Reflection

Visibility was the foundation. Coaching was the next frontier.

V1 gave analysts something they’d never had: a real signal. They could see which conversations failed, where users pushed back, and what the feedback said. That was the prerequisite — you can’t improve what you can’t see.

But visibility alone wasn’t enough. Analysts could identify individual failures. The harder questions remained: How do you recognize a pattern across hundreds of failures? How do you know which coaching would move the needle? How do you reduce the manual effort of improving Spotter week over week?

Those questions pointed directly at Memory — a new layer that would let Spotter build and update its own knowledge over time, moving coaching from a manual analyst task to an intelligent, compounding system. Monitoring built the case for it. Memory became the next bet.

What I’d do differently: I’d have instrumented success metrics into V1 from day one — adoption of the monitor, time-to-find a failing conversation, coaching actions taken. The visibility I built for analysts is exactly the visibility I should have built for the team measuring whether the feature worked.

09Experience & learnings

My experience & learnings

This was my first end-to-end strategic project, spanning product strategy, information architecture, and UX design. What began as a request for conversation visibility evolved into a broader challenge of defining what monitoring should mean for Spotter.

As the project progressed, I explored several directions including auditing, benchmarking, and collaboration workflows. While each addressed legitimate user needs, they also expanded the scope beyond the core problem we were trying to solve.

The most important lesson was realizing that deeper understanding often leads to simpler solutions. The final experience was significantly more focused than many of the concepts explored along the way, yet it better supported what analysts actually needed: finding issues, understanding them, and improving Spotter.

Visibility is not the same as monitoring

Exposing conversations was only the starting point. Effective monitoring required helping analysts identify issues, understand them, and take action.

Exploration is valuable even when it doesn't ship

Many of the strongest ideas explored during the project never became part of the final solution. Their value was in helping define the boundaries of the product and clarifying what belonged in V1.

The workflow mattered more than any individual screen

The final solution succeeded because it connected discovery, investigation, and coaching into a single workflow. The value came from reducing friction between these steps, not from any individual feature.

10What I'd do differently

What I'd do differently

Exploration creates clarity, but it needs boundaries

Some of the most valuable insights came from ideas that never shipped. Exploring adjacent problems helped us understand the opportunity space and make more informed decisions. However, every new direction increased complexity, expanded scope, and demanded additional time for validation and design. The challenge was knowing when to continue exploring and when to commit to a focused approach.
